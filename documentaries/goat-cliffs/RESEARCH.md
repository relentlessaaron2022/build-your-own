# The Cliff File: research notes (verified, with sources)

Only facts below appear in the script. Anything sources disagree on is left out or phrased to the safe side.

| Claim used in script | Source |
|---|---|
| Mountain goat hooves split into two toes that spread; soft, flexible inner pads grip rock | NPS Glacier, Mountain Goat resource brief 2019 (nps.gov/rlc/crown/upload/Mountain-Goat_2019.pdf) |
| Pads are soft and rubbery with a hard outer rim | NPS North Cascades (nps.gov/noca/learn/nature/mountain-goats.htm); NPS Mount Rainier (nps.gov/mora/learn/nature/hoofed-mammals.htm) |
| Climbing where others can't follow is the goat's main defense | NPS Kenai Fjords (nps.gov/kefj/learn/nature/mountain-goat.htm) |
| Not a true goat: a goat-antelope, a relative of the chamois | Britannica, "mountain goat" |
| Large males can top 120 kg (260 lb); females about 60–90 kg | Britannica |
| Mothers give birth on steep, secluded ledges in late May or June | NWF, "Life on the Edge" (1991); Alaska Dept. of Fish & Game |
| A newborn is on its feet within minutes | NWF, "King of the Mountain" (1997) |
| Golden eagles try to strike kids off ledges | Bitterroot mountain goat study excerpt (University Press of Colorado, muse.jhu.edu) |
| 12-year study: eagles attacked goats in about 1.9% of active-eagle sightings, never succeeded; mothers always defended their young | Hamel & Côté 2009, Western North American Naturalist 69(1) |
| The cougar is "probably best adapted" of the goat's predators | Bitterroot study excerpt (muse.jhu.edu) |
| Where predation was measured, grizzlies and wolves mattered most | same |
| 40–60% of kids die in their first winter, probably from starvation; falls and avalanches matter but are not the main limit | British Columbia Ministry of Environment, mountain goat brochure (env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/mtngoat.pdf) |
| Alpine ibex climb the Cingino Dam (Piedmont, Italy), about 49 m tall, near-vertical (park officials estimated ~80°) to lick mineral salts | Wikipedia "Cingino Dam"; Outside; NZ Herald |
| The climbers are mostly females and young; big adult males weren't seen doing it | Outside (Lovari quote) |

Left out on purpose: the "12-foot jump" (only one source), "kids climb within hours" (NPS says 10–14 days), any cougar kill numbers (none published that we found).
