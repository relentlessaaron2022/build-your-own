Look closely. A cockroach. A giant, by any measure, next to the creatures around it. And yet, one by one, then dozens at a time, the ants arrive.
How is that even possible? The ants are not stronger. Not one of them could win this alone. But none of them is alone.
When the little ones come together, even giants fall. This is the mystery, and the magic, of the ant kingdom.
Start with the numbers, because the numbers are hard to believe.
In 2022, a team of scientists gathered nearly five hundred studies of ants, from every continent and every major habitat, and they counted. Their estimate: about twenty quadrillion ants alive on Earth right now. A twenty, followed by fifteen zeros.
And they called that number conservative.
That is roughly two and a half million ants for every human being on the planet. Put them all on a scale, and ants outweigh every wild bird and every wild mammal on Earth, combined.
Scientists have named more than twelve thousand species. They live on every continent except Antarctica. They were here long before us, and they have been working together the entire time.
So here is the question. Who is in charge?
The answer is the strangest part of the story. Nobody.
The queen does not give orders. She lays eggs. That is her job, and it is a big one. But she does not command an army, and she does not draw a plan.
Instead, the colony runs on signals.
Ants talk through chemicals called pheromones. When a worker finds food, she lays a scent trail on the way home. Other workers follow it. If the food is good, they lay trail too, and the path grows stronger. When the food runs out, nobody reinforces the trail, and it fades away.
No meeting. No memo. The best route simply gets louder.
And when danger appears, a different signal goes out. An alarm. What started as a few workers can become dozens in minutes.
Then there are the tools. Ants carry them on their faces. They are called mandibles. Jaws that grip, bite, cut and carry. One set of mandibles is small. A thousand sets, working the same target, are something else.
That is what you are seeing in that video. Often, the insect is already weakened or injured. The ants do not need to be bigger. They only need to be more, and they need to move as one.
Scientists have a name for this. Collective behavior. Each ant follows simple, local rules. Together, the colony solves problems that no single ant could ever understand.
To see how far that idea goes, open four case files.
Case file one. The army.
In the rainforests of Central and South America, one species of army ant lives in colonies of hundreds of thousands of workers. They have no permanent home. Each night, they build one, out of their own bodies, linking legs and jaws into a living nest around their queen. They call it a bivouac.
By day, they raid. A swarm front up to ten meters wide sweeps across the forest floor, and almost everything small in its path either runs or is carried away.
Case file two. The farmers.
Long before human beings planted a single seed, ants were farming. By many scientific estimates, ants began growing fungus around fifty million years ago.
Leafcutter ants carry pieces of leaves back home, not to eat them, but to feed underground gardens of fungus. The fungus feeds the colony. The colony tends the fungus. A partnership, older than any human civilization.
Case file three. The raft.
When floods come, a single fire ant struggles in the water. But a colony does something remarkable. The ants grip each other with their claws, their jaws and the pads on their feet, and they weave themselves into a living raft. Thousands of them, floating together, with the queen and the young kept safe on board. Researchers have watched these rafts stay afloat for days, even weeks.
No ant built that raft. Every ant is the raft.
Case file four. The empire.
A little brown ant from South America, the Argentine ant, arrived in Europe about a century ago, probably on ships. Today, one of its supercolonies stretches about six thousand kilometers along the coast, from Italy to the Atlantic coast of Spain.
Inside that supercolony, ants from nests thousands of kilometers apart treat each other as family. They do not fight. But when they meet ants from a rival supercolony, they fight to the death.
Think about that. One of the largest cooperative societies in Europe is made entirely of little ones.
So what does all of this mean for us?
The biggest creature does not always win. The strongest individual does not always survive. And having every advantage in the world means very little when you are facing a united, organized force.
Think about that in your community. In your family. In your business. In any movement that ever changed anything.
Nobody needs to see the whole mission. Each one only needs to do their part, follow the signal, and hold on to the ones beside them.
One ant is small. A thousand ants are a force.
Nature keeps reminding us that unity can turn the powerless into the powerful.
And maybe that is the real magic. Not that the little ones can bring down a giant. But that together, they become something much bigger than themselves.
