Meet the neighbors who help Ricky make the song.

Book 1 is not a solo. The girl on the stoop, the man with the bucket, and the kid with the bike bell each get a real part to play — and that is the point.

The published comic does not give these neighbors first names, so this page does not invent any. It introduces them the way the book does, which is also a useful talking point with a child: you do not have to be the star of the block to belong in the song.

The man with the bucketAn older man in a flat cap sits on a stool and drums on an upside-down metal bucket. BOOM, BOOM. He is the one who tells Ricky, “Everything has a beat, young man,” and later, when Ricky figures out the order, “Now you're thinking like a drummer.”
The kid with the bike bellA kid in a red helmet rides a blue bike down the street and rings a small bell: RING, RING. He stops, asks Ricky what he is doing, and then joins the band.
The girl on the stoopA girl with a purple headband and hoop earrings claps on the brownstone steps: CLAP, CLAP. She asks, “Can I be in your song?” Ricky puts her on the clap, and she stays in the story all the way to the high-five at sunset.
The rest of the blockA city bus sighs at the corner. A man sweeps in front of a corner store. A dog barks behind a fence. Later, neighbors lean out of windows, a woman taps a watering can, a kid drums on a box, and a grandma shakes her keys. The song gets bigger because Ricky keeps listening.

If you are buying this for a child, this is one of the quiet strengths of the book. Ricky leads, but he does not hoard the music. The girl asks to join and is told yes. The first attempt fails because nobody takes turns. The second attempt works because they do. That is a neighborhood lesson, and it is also a family-room lesson.
