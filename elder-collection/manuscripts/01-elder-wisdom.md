# Introduction

You already know the voice I'm talking about. It came from the porch or the kitchen or the front pew, and it usually started with "Let me tell you something." You were young. You nodded. You didn't listen.

Then life came and collected on every lesson you skipped.

That's how most of us learn what elders know. Not when they say it. Years later, standing in a parking lot or a hospital hallway or an empty apartment, when the words come back in their exact tone of voice, and we finally understand them.

This book is for the people who carried that wisdom and the people who are only now ready to receive it. The faces in these pages are drawn with a little exaggeration (big glasses, big hair, bigger opinions) because that's how memory draws the ones we love. Every one of them has the same quiet confidence. They aren't guessing. They've been there.

So sit down. Pour something warm. Let them tell you something.

# Chapter 1

Aunt Birdie kept a pencil in her bun. Nobody alive had seen her write with it.

The porch was her classroom, and she didn't take attendance. You came up the three steps and sat where she pointed, usually the low stool by the rail, so she could look down at you over the top of those wire glasses. Then she waited. That was the first lesson, and most of the children on Calloway Street failed it every summer: Birdie didn't talk until you stopped talking.

"You came up here with something on your mind," she'd say. "Put it down."

So you put it down. The boy who stole from the corner store. The girl whose mama wasn't coming back from Detroit. The teenager who thought he was too smart for school and wanted somebody to agree with him. Birdie listened to every one of them the same way, hands folded in her lap, rocking a little, saying nothing until the story ran out of road.

Pops Earl lived two doors down and liked to say that Birdie never gave advice. "She just asks you one question," he'd say, "and you walk home with your own answer stuck in your shoe."

He was right. To the boy who stole, she asked, "Who'd you want to see you do it?" He said nobody. She waited. He said his older brother. She nodded like that settled it, and in a way it did. To the teenager who was too smart for school, she asked, "Who's going to pay you to be smart in your own head?" He laughed. Then he stopped laughing.

There's a reason the porch worked when the classroom didn't. A porch sits halfway between the house and the street, between private and public, between who you are at home and who you pretend to be outside. On a porch, you could tell the truth and still leave whenever you wanted. Birdie never kept anybody. She just made it easy to stay.

What she understood, and what most of us figure out too late, is that wisdom is mostly patience with a good chair. She didn't know more facts than the teachers at Lincoln Elementary. She knew more people. Eighty-four years of watching folks make the same five mistakes in different shoes. When you've seen a story end enough times, you can hear the ending coming from the first sentence.

The summer I'm thinking of, a girl named Toya came up the steps every afternoon for two weeks and never said a word. She sat on the stool. Birdie rocked. The ice in their glasses melted. On the fifteenth day, Toya finally said, "I don't think I'm smart."

Birdie took the pencil out of her bun for the first time anybody could remember and handed it to her.

"Then write down what you notice," she said. "Smart is just noticing, written down."

Toya is a nurse practitioner now. She still has the pencil.

You had a porch too, even if it was a stoop, a barbershop chair or the back seat of somebody's Buick. Somebody sat there waiting for you to stop talking. The question this chapter wants to leave you with is simple, and it's Birdie's: what did they ask you that you're still answering?

# Chapter 2

Pops Earl didn't talk about 1974. Miss Mavis did it for him.

"That was the year the plant closed," she'd say, setting the cornbread down like it was evidence. "That was the year this man learned how to fix every single thing in this house, because we couldn't pay anybody to do it."

Pops would grunt and reach for the butter. But if you watched him close, you'd see his hand go to his shirt pocket, where he kept a small notebook with a pencil clipped to the spine. In 1974 that notebook held the budget. Every dollar, every week, written in a hand so careful it looked typed. Fifty years later, it still held something. Phone numbers. A grocery list. A note to call his sister.

The hard years teach in a different voice than the easy ones. Easy years teach you what you like. Hard years teach you what you can do without, and which people stay in the room when there's nothing on the table.

Mavis says the hardest lesson that year wasn't money. It was pride. Pops had been the man who paid for things. Now he was the man who went to the church pantry on Thursday with his hat pulled low. He hated it. She didn't make him talk about it. She walked with him every Thursday, and every Thursday she talked loud and easy to everybody in line, so nobody had room to stare at him.

"Shame grows in the quiet," she said. "So I made noise."

Ask anybody who came through a hard season with somebody beside them and they'll tell you the same thing. They don't remember the details of the struggle. They remember who walked with them on Thursdays.
