# Introduction

You already know the voice I'm talking about. It came from the porch or the kitchen or the front pew, and it usually started with "Let me tell you something." You were young. You nodded. You didn't listen.

Then life came and collected on every lesson you skipped.

That's how most of us learn what elders know. Not when they say it. Years later, standing in a parking lot or a hospital hallway or an empty apartment, the words come back in their exact tone of voice, and we finally understand them.

This book is for the people who carried that wisdom and for the people who are only now ready to receive it. The elders in these pages live on a street called Calloway. You won't find it on a map, but you've been there. It's the block where everybody's grandmother could correct you and everybody's grandfather knew your daddy. The faces are drawn with a little exaggeration, big glasses, big hair, bigger opinions, because that's how memory draws the ones we love.

Read it in order or open it anywhere. Every chapter is a story, and every picture carries a line worth keeping. Some of those lines will sound like somebody you know. That's the point.

So sit down. Pour something warm. Let them tell you something.

# Chapter 1

Aunt Birdie kept a pencil in her bun. Nobody alive had seen her write with it.

The porch was her classroom, and she didn't take attendance. You came up the three steps and sat where she pointed, usually the low stool by the rail, so she could look down at you over the top of those wire glasses. Then she waited. That was the first lesson, and most of the children on Calloway Street failed it every summer. Birdie didn't talk until you stopped talking.

"You came up here with something on your mind," she'd say. "Put it down."

So you put it down. The boy who stole from the corner store. The girl whose mama wasn't coming back from Detroit. The teenager who thought he was too smart for school and wanted somebody to agree with him. Birdie listened to every one of them the same way, hands folded in her lap, rocking a little, saying nothing until the story ran out of road.

Pops Earl lived two doors down and liked to say that Birdie never gave advice. "She asks you one question," he'd say, "and you walk home with your own answer stuck in your shoe."

He was right. To the boy who stole, she asked, "Who'd you want to see you do it?" He said nobody. She waited. He said his older brother. She nodded like that settled it, and in a way it did. To the teenager who was too smart for school, she asked, "Who's going to pay you to be smart in your own head?" He laughed. Then he stopped laughing.

There's a reason the porch worked when the classroom didn't. A porch sits halfway between the house and the street, between who you are at home and who you pretend to be outside. On a porch you could tell the truth and still leave whenever you wanted. Birdie never kept anybody. She just made it easy to stay.

What she understood, and what most of us figure out too late, is that wisdom is mostly patience with a good chair. She didn't know more facts than the teachers at Lincoln Elementary. She knew more people. Eighty-four years of watching folks make the same five mistakes in different shoes. When you've seen a story end enough times, you can hear the ending coming from the first sentence.

The summer I'm thinking of, a girl named Toya came up the steps every afternoon for two weeks and never said a word. She sat on the stool. Birdie rocked. The ice in their glasses melted. On the fifteenth day, Toya finally said, "I don't think I'm smart."

Birdie took the pencil out of her bun for the first time anybody could remember and handed it to her.

"Then write down what you notice," she said. "Smart is just noticing, written down."

Toya is a nurse practitioner now. She still has the pencil.

You had a porch too, even if it was a stoop, a barbershop chair or the back seat of somebody's Buick. Somebody sat there waiting for you to stop talking. Birdie's question is the one this chapter leaves with you: what did they ask you that you're still answering?

# Chapter 2

Pops Earl didn't talk about 1974. Miss Mavis did it for him.

"That was the year the plant closed," she'd say, setting the cornbread down like it was evidence. "That was the year this man learned how to fix every single thing in this house, because we couldn't pay anybody to do it."

Pops would grunt and reach for the butter. But if you watched him close, you'd see his hand drift to his shirt pocket, where he kept a small notebook with a pencil clipped to the spine. In 1974 that notebook held the budget. Every dollar, every week, written in a hand so careful it looked typed. Rent. Light bill. Gas for the Pontiac, which mostly sat. Two dollars a week marked "church," and he never once skipped it, even the week the refrigerator quit.

Fifty years later the notebook still lives in that pocket. Phone numbers now. A grocery list. A note to call his sister on Sundays. Different notebook, same habit. He'll tell you the habit is what saved them, not the money.

The hard years teach in a different voice than the easy ones. Easy years teach you what you like. Hard years teach you what you can do without, and who stays in the room when there's nothing on the table.

Mavis says the hardest lesson that year wasn't money. It was pride. Pops had been the man who paid for things. Now he was the man in line at the church pantry on Thursday mornings with his hat pulled low. He hated it so much he stopped talking at dinner. She didn't make him talk. She walked with him every Thursday, and every Thursday she talked loud and easy to everybody in line, about the weather and the choir and whose grandbaby had a new tooth, so nobody had room to stare at him.

"Shame grows in the quiet," she says. "So I made noise."

Across the street, Aunt Hattie's family was going through it too. Her husband worked the same line at the same plant. The two families started sharing. Hattie had a garden. Mavis had a sewing machine. Pops could fix anything with a motor. By the end of that winter, four houses on Calloway were running like one, trading collards for hemmed pants for a working furnace.

Nobody called it a program. Nobody wrote a grant. They just did it, because it was the only way through.

Coach Clarence, who was a young man then, still laughs about it. "We were broke as a joke," he says, holding his coffee with both hands like it might get away. "Best year of my life. I didn't know it yet."

That's the strange gift of a hard season. You don't recognize it while you're in it. You recognize it later, when you notice that every good habit you have, every friend you'd call at three in the morning, every skill in your hands, came from the year everything fell apart.

Pops went back to work in 1976, at a different plant across town. He never went back to living without the notebook.

Ask anybody who came through a hard season with somebody beside them and they'll tell you the same thing. They don't remember the details of the struggle. They remember who walked with them on Thursdays.

Maybe you're in your 1974 right now. If you are, here's what the elders on Calloway would want you to know. Write it down. Every dollar, every week. Find the folks on your street who are going through it too, and trade what you have. Let somebody make noise for you. And keep the receipts, because one day you'll want to remember exactly how you made it.

# Chapter 3

Grandma Pearl kept the family finances in envelopes.

Not a bank app. Not a spreadsheet. Envelopes, the long white kind, each one labeled in ballpoint and stacked in a shoebox on the top shelf of the hall closet. LIGHT. GAS. RENT. CHURCH. CHRISTMAS. And one that just said MINE.

When her grandson Dante turned sixteen and got his first paycheck from the Kroger on Fifth, she sat him down at the kitchen table, slid the shoebox across, and said, "Show me where it's going."

Dante laughed. He had an app. She waited. He opened it, and she watched him scroll, and she didn't say a word until he scrolled past three food deliveries in one week.

"Mm," she said.

That was all. Just "mm." Dante closed the app.

Pearl's system was simple. The day the money came in, it went into the envelopes before it could go anywhere else. God got paid first. You got paid second. The light bill got paid third, because "you can't pray in the dark and you can't save in the dark either." Whatever was left was for living. If an envelope ran out, you stopped spending from it. There was no borrowing from CHRISTMAS to cover GAS. That was the whole rule, and she'd kept it for sixty years.

"The envelope can't lie to you," she told Dante. "Your phone can. It shows you a number and the number feels like money. Paper feels like what it is."

Mr. Jasper, who sat with Dante the next week, came at it from another side. Jasper had worked at a credit union for thirty-one years and seen every way a person could drown. He told Dante that interest was rent. "You're renting money you don't have," he said, "to buy things you don't need, from people who don't know your name." He let that sit. "And the rent never stops."

Then he showed him the other side. He took a pencil and drew a line that started low and curved up and up. "That's what happens when you're the landlord," he said. "When your money pays you rent."

Money was one leg of the lesson. Manners were the other two.

Every Sunday before church, Mother Gloria stood at her front door and inspected whichever grandchild was going with her. Collar straight. Shoes clean. Shirt tucked. She'd lick her thumb and fix a cowlick and the child would squirm, and she'd say the same thing every week: "People decide who you are before you open your mouth. Make it easy for them to decide right."

You can argue with that. A lot of young folks do. They'll tell you the world shouldn't judge by appearance, and they're right. But Mother Gloria grew up in a world that judged her by appearance every day of her life, and she learned that a straight collar was a kind of armor. It didn't make her smaller. It made her harder to dismiss.

Miss Mavis taught the boys on Calloway to tie a tie. Uncle Otis taught them to shake a hand. "Firm, not mean," he'd say. "Look at the man's eyes. Say your whole name."

None of it was complicated. All of it was a system. Money in envelopes. Collar straight. Whole name, eyes up. The elders understood something we tend to forget. Discipline isn't punishment. It's freedom you build in advance.

Dante is twenty-four now. He still uses the app. But on the first of every month, he moves money into separate accounts with names like LIGHT and GAS and MINE.

He told his grandmother. She said, "Mm."

This time it meant something else.

# Chapter 4

Granddaddy Booker and Miss Lula Mae have been married sixty-six years, and she has every letter he ever wrote her.

They're in a butter cookie tin on the dresser, the blue kind every family seems to own. Most of them are from 1958 and 1959, when the Army sent him to a base in West Germany and she stayed home in a rented room, working at a laundry and waiting. He wrote every day. Some days it was three pages. Some days it was one line: "Cold here. Thinking about you. B."

She wrote back every day too. His letters to her made it home. Hers to him didn't. He lost them in a move in 1971 and has never forgiven himself. "That's the only thing I ever lost that I'd go back for," he says.

People ask them the secret, the way people always ask couples who've lasted. Lula Mae has a ready answer. "There isn't one," she says. "There's ten thousand little ones."

Then she lists them, ticking them off on fingers bent by age. He warms up the car in winter. She doesn't ask where he's been, because he always tells her before she can. He brings her coffee in bed, black, every morning, and has since 1960. She laughs at the same three jokes he's been telling for six decades. They go to bed angry sometimes. "That advice is foolishness," she says. "Sometimes you need to sleep on it. You just have to wake up and be kind first thing."

Across Calloway, Uncle Otis and Grandma Pearl have a different style. They argue the way some couples dance, loud and fast and completely in step. They've been arguing about the thermostat since the Carter administration. But at night, when the house is quiet, you might catch them on the couch with their foreheads touching, saying nothing at all.

Mr. Jasper and Aunt Hattie walk every evening after dinner, arm in arm, down to the end of the block and back. He points at things: a new car, a cat, a crack in the sidewalk that wasn't there yesterday. She says, "Mm-hm," and leans in. They've had that walk so long it's become a kind of conversation all by itself.

What all of them understand, and what the movies get wrong, is that lasting love isn't one big feeling. It's maintenance. It's the boring, beautiful work of showing up the same way every day until the showing up becomes the love.

The young folks on Calloway find this a little disappointing at first. They want fireworks. Booker understands. He had fireworks once, in a dance hall on Ninth Street in 1956, when he saw a girl in a yellow dress and forgot his own name. "Fireworks get you in the door," he says. "Then you've got to build a house."

Lula Mae still has the yellow dress, too. It's in the back of the closet, behind the winter coats. She can't fit in it anymore, and she doesn't care. Some things you keep for proof.

If you're loved by somebody right now, today might be a good day to do one of the ten thousand little things. Warm up the car. Bring the coffee. Laugh at the old joke.

And if you have letters, keep them somewhere safe. A butter cookie tin works fine.

# Chapter 5

At eighty-nine, Granddaddy Booker planted an oak tree.

He did it on a Saturday in April, at the edge of the community garden at the end of Calloway Street. He couldn't dig the hole himself anymore, so his great-grandson Marcus dug it while Booker sat in a folding chair and gave instructions. "Deeper. Wider. You're digging for a tree, not a tulip."

Marcus, who is fourteen and good at math, did the calculation out loud while he dug. A white oak takes twenty years to make real shade. Booker would be one hundred and nine.

Booker laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

"Baby," he said, "I'm not planting it for me."

That's the patience the elders mean. Not the patience of waiting for something you want. The patience of doing something right even though you'll never see the payoff. They plant trees for people they won't meet. They save for grandchildren who aren't born yet. They pray for things that may take longer than one lifetime to arrive.

Aunt Hattie teaches the same lesson with a fishing pole. Every summer she takes one grandchild at a time to the short dock at Miller's Pond. No phones. No talking above a whisper. Just two lines in the water and a long, slow afternoon.

The children hate it for the first hour. They fidget. They check the line every thirty seconds. They ask when they can go home. Hattie doesn't argue. She just says, "Fish can tell when you're in a hurry."

By the second hour, something changes. The child gets quiet. Starts noticing the dragonflies, the way the light moves on the water, the old man across the pond who's been sitting just as still. By the third hour, most of them don't want to leave, whether they caught anything or not.

"The fish was never the point," Hattie says.

Patience is a muscle. That's the phrase Mr. Jasper uses, and he should know, because he's been playing chess against Aunt Hattie every Tuesday night for twenty-two years and has won exactly once. He keeps playing. The one win was in 2011. He remembers every move.

"The game teaches you to sit with a problem," he says. "Young people want to solve everything in one move. Most problems take twelve. Some take a lifetime. You have to be willing to look at the board a long time before you touch anything."

Hattie's take is shorter. "He moves too fast," she says. "Every Tuesday. Twenty-two years."

We live in a world that sells speed. Faster delivery. Faster answers. Faster results, faster healing, faster success. And there's nothing wrong with fast when fast is right. But some of the best things in life refuse to be rushed. A tree. A marriage. A child becoming who they are. A grief turning into something you can carry.

The elders aren't slow because they're old. A lot of them were slow on purpose long before their knees made them. They figured out early that the hurry is usually where the mistakes live.

Booker's oak is about knee high now. Marcus checks on it after school, though he'd never admit it to his friends. He waters it in August. He told his great-granddaddy the leaves came in.

Booker smiled, and didn't say a word, and looked out the window toward the end of the block, as if he could already see the shade.

# Chapter 6

Uncle Otis has never said "I love you" out loud. Not once, in seventy-four years, to anybody.

Grandma Pearl doesn't mind. She learned his language a long time ago.

"He warms up the car," she says. "Every morning in winter, he goes out at six and starts it so it's warm when I get in. Fifty-one years. That's 'I love you.' He checks my tires. That's 'I love you.' He calls the children on their birthdays and talks about the weather for one minute and hangs up. That's 'I love you, too, and I'm proud of you, and I don't know how to say it.'"

A lot of elders speak this language. Many of them grew up in homes where feelings weren't discussed. Some of them grew up in a country that punished them for showing too much of anything. Fear, anger, grief, even joy. They learned to keep the important things behind the eyes and let the hands do the talking.

So they fixed things. They cooked. They showed up. They slipped twenty dollars into your palm at the end of a visit and closed your fingers around it so you couldn't argue. They stood at the back of your graduation, too far away for a picture, and left before you could find them in the crowd.

If you grew up loved by one of these people, you may have spent years wishing they'd just say it. And then, one day, you understood that they had been saying it the whole time.

Pops Earl stands at the window every evening with his hands folded behind his back and looks at the sky for a long time. Miss Mavis asked him once what he was looking at. He said, "Just checking." She asked, "Checking for what?" He thought about it and said, "That everybody got home."

Mr. Jasper has a different way. He writes letters to his brother Leon, who passed in 1998. One a month, in a leather journal, with a fountain pen Leon gave him for his fiftieth birthday. He's never shown anybody what's in them. Aunt Hattie has never asked. "That's between him and Leon," she says. "Some talking you do with the door closed."

Aunt Birdie does the same thing for different reasons. Her journal is full of the people who came up her porch steps. Not their secrets. Just what she noticed. Toya bit her nails when she lied. Dante's laugh got deeper the summer he turned fifteen. She writes it down because she knows that one day she won't be there to remember, and somebody should.

There's a kind of wisdom in that silence that's easy to miss. The elders know words can be cheap. Anybody can say "I love you." Not everybody will warm up the car at six in the morning in January for fifty-one years.

Still, if you're the one who learned to talk about your feelings, don't let anybody tell you that's a weakness. You're carrying something forward too. Maybe the work of your generation is to say out loud what theirs could only show. And maybe the work is also to learn their language. To notice the warm car. The tire check. The twenty in your palm.

Grandma Pearl got sick two winters ago. Pneumonia, a week in the hospital. Otis sat by the bed every day and read the newspaper out loud, the whole paper, every section, even the classifieds.

On the last day, as they got her into the wheelchair to go home, he leaned down and said something in her ear. She has never told anybody what it was.

She just smiles when you ask.

# Chapter 7

There's a garden at the end of Calloway Street where a crack house used to be.

The house burned in 1989. The lot sat empty for eleven years, full of broken glass and tires and worse. The city said it would clean it up. The city said that every year. In the summer of 2000, Deacon Ross got tired of waiting.

He went door to door with a clipboard. Miss Mavis typed the petition on her old Smith-Corona, because she didn't trust computers and said they made everything look like a bill. Forty-one names. Deacon took it to the city council and stood at the microphone in his charcoal three-piece suit and read every single name out loud. It took six minutes. Nobody interrupted him.

They got the lot.

The first summer was just cleanup. The men hauled out tires and glass and the burned frame of a couch. Granddaddy Booker, who was sixty-three then and strong as a mule, broke up the old foundation with a sledgehammer. Coach Clarence brought his summer league team every Saturday morning and told them it was conditioning.

The second summer, they planted.

Brother Walter ran the seed program from his wheelchair. He'd been a machinist before the accident, and he still had a machinist's hands: precise, patient, never wasted. He sat at a folding table under an umbrella and sorted seeds into little envelopes, labeled in perfect block capitals. TOMATO, BETTER BOY. COLLARD, GEORGIA. OKRA, CLEMSON SPINELESS. Every child who came by got an envelope and a job.

"You don't get the food unless you get dirty," he told them. "That's the deal."

Twenty-six years later, the garden feeds about forty families every summer. There's a shed now, painted teal, and a rain barrel, and a bench with a brass plate that says IN HONOR OF THE HANDS THAT BUILT THIS. Miss Vivian's granddaughter runs the Saturday program. Pops Earl's grandson is planting next to Deacon Ross's grandson, the same way their grandfathers once worked side by side.

The elders call this kind of work "building," and they mean it in more than one way. You build a garden. You build a family. You build a name, slowly, one kept promise at a time. You build a church, a business, a block. Everything they have, they built, and most of it was built with their hands before it was built with anything else.

Granddaddy Booker built the rocking chair on Aunt Birdie's porch, the one in the first chapter of this book. He made it in 1972 out of white oak, no nails, every joint pegged. It has outlasted two porches, one hurricane and one Birdie's nephew who tried to fix the squeak with WD-40. "You don't oil wood," Booker said. "You leave it alone and let it speak."

There's a reason the old ones put so much weight on work you can touch. When you're building with your hands, you can't lie to yourself. The joint fits or it doesn't. The tomato grows or it doesn't. The roof keeps out rain or it doesn't. There's an honesty in it that a lot of modern work has lost.

Brother Walter puts it this way. "When I was young, I thought the work was the thing you did to get paid," he says. "Turns out, the work is the thing you leave."

Look around your house tonight. Somebody built something you're using. A table, a recipe, a habit, the house itself. Find out who. Say their name.

And then go build something your grandchildren will use.

# Chapter 8

Every Wednesday night for twenty years, Mother Gloria and Coach Clarence have argued about God at her kitchen table.

It started at a church fish fry in 2006. Clarence came because the fish was good, not because of the church. Gloria asked him, the way she asks everybody, whether he'd be at service on Sunday. He said no. She asked why not. He said he wasn't sure anybody was listening.

Anybody else would have let it go. Gloria invited him to dinner.

He came on Wednesday. She made smothered pork chops. They argued until eleven at night. He came back the next Wednesday, and the next, and that was twenty years ago.

Neither one has converted the other. Clarence still doesn't come on Sundays. Gloria still prays for him by name, out loud, every night. "I tell God about him," she says. "He knows about Clarence. Clarence just hasn't been formally introduced."

What happens at that table is something rare. Two people who disagree about the biggest question there is, and who love each other anyway. They never shout. They interrupt each other constantly. They quote scripture and philosophy and baseball statistics. Miss Odessa sometimes comes by and reads in the corner and pretends she isn't listening. She is. She says it's better than television.

"Here's what I know," Gloria says. "Doubt is not the opposite of faith. Doubt is the doorway. Everybody I ever respected walked through it at least once."

Clarence's version is plainer. "I don't know if anybody's listening," he says. "But I know Gloria is. That's enough to keep me coming back on Wednesdays."

The test came in 2019, when Clarence's wife, Denise, passed after a long illness. He didn't come to the table for three Wednesdays. On the fourth, Gloria didn't wait. She went to him.

She brought a candle in a brass holder and set it on his kitchen table and lit it. She didn't preach. She didn't quote anything. She just sat there with him while it burned down, two hours, and when it went out, she lit another one.

At the repast after the funeral, Grandma Pearl and Coach Clarence stood together holding candles while the choir sang "I'll Fly Away." Pearl says he sang every word. "He knows the words," she says. "He's always known the words. He just likes to argue."

There's a lesson here that the elders understand better than most of us. Faith and doubt can live in the same house. They can sit at the same table. They can share a meal and a candle and a long life, and neither one has to win.

Miss Lula Mae reads her Bible every morning with her coffee, the same worn one she's had since 1951. She's underlined it so much some pages are more ink than paper. She'll tell you she still has questions. "I've been reading this book seventy-five years," she says. "If I didn't have questions, I wasn't paying attention."

Maybe you sit on one side of that table. Maybe you sit on the other. Maybe you're like most of us and you move back and forth depending on the week. The elders on Calloway Street would say that's all right.

Just keep coming back on Wednesdays.

# Chapter 9

We asked the elders of Calloway Street one question: if you could go back and talk to yourself at twenty, what would you say?

Some of them laughed. Some of them went quiet for a long time. A few of them asked if they had to keep it clean. Here's what they told us.

Brother Walter got his college degree at fifty-eight. Business administration, night classes, six years, two of them after the accident that put him in the wheelchair. He has the diploma framed in his front room where everybody who walks in can see it. "I'd tell my young self it's not too late," he says. "And I'd tell him it's not too early either. I waited thirty years thinking there'd be a better time. There's never a better time. There's just now."

Mr. Reggie keeps his old photo albums on the coffee table and turns the pages slowly when company comes. He's in a lot of them, always the sharpest-dressed man in the picture. Cream suits, two-tone shoes, hats tilted just so. "I'd tell him to slow down and actually look at people," he says. "I spent my twenties trying to be seen. I should've spent them seeing."

Pops Earl peered at the question for a while, then said one word. "Save." Then he thought about it some more and added two more. "Save. Call home."

Miss Odessa and Pops laughed for ten straight minutes over a photograph from 1969, the two of them at a house party, both in plaid, both with hair so big it went out of the frame. When they got their breath back, Odessa said, "I'd tell her not to worry so much about what people think. Look at us. Everybody who ever judged that outfit is gone, and we're still laughing."

Mother Gloria said she'd tell herself that forgiveness is for you, not them. "I carried my sister's words for twenty years," she says. "She forgot she ever said them. I was the only one paying rent on that grudge."

Mr. Reggie, again, unable to resist: "Wear sunscreen. I didn't know we needed it. We need it."

Granddaddy Booker said he'd tell himself to write his wife's letters down twice, so he'd have a copy. "I lost hers," he says. "Sixty-some years and I still think about it."

The most common answers, when we added them up, were these. Save more. Worry less. Call your mother. Forgive faster. Go back to school. Take care of your teeth. Tell people you love them while they can hear it. Nobody said they wished they'd worked more. Nobody said they wished they'd stayed angry longer. Several said they wished they'd danced more.

Aunt Birdie gave the shortest answer and the best one. She thought about it on her porch for two days. Then she called us and said, "Tell her she's going to be fine. And tell her she won't believe you."

That's the gift, isn't it? You can't go back. But somebody younger than you is standing right now where you once stood, scared and certain and wrong about half of it. You can tell them. They won't believe you. Tell them anyway.

One day they'll be on a porch somewhere, saying the same thing.

# Chapter 10

Aunt Hattie's mother left her a pair of sewing scissors, a recipe for chess pie, and one instruction: "Don't keep it. Pass it."

The scissors are heavy and black-handled and sharp enough to cut silk like water. Hattie's mother got them in 1931 from the woman she cleaned house for, who was moving away and didn't want to pack them. They've cut three generations of Easter dresses, two wedding gowns, and one christening gown that's been worn by nine babies.

Last Christmas, Hattie wrapped them in tissue paper, put them in a box with the recipe card, and handed them to her granddaughter Simone, who is twenty-two and studying fashion design. Simone cried before she opened it. She knew what it was from the weight.

"Don't keep it," Hattie told her. "Pass it."

That's the whole idea of this last chapter, and maybe of this whole book. Wisdom isn't something you own. It's something that moves through you.

Pops Earl did his passing in the spring. He gave his grandson Darnell a box, and inside the box was a notebook. Not the one from his pocket. The first one. The 1974 one, with every dollar of the hard year written in that careful hand. On the inside cover, Pops had written a single line. "This is how we made it. Now you know how."

Darnell is a father himself now. He read the notebook at the kitchen table that night, every page, and then he went to the store and bought a small notebook of his own.

Coach Clarence wears a key on a ribbon around his neck, the key to the youth center on Twelfth Street, which he's run for thirty years. He's seventy-two. He knows he won't run it forever. So every Saturday, he lets one of the older kids open up. The ribbon goes around their neck for the day. They stand a little taller. That's the plan. One of them will keep the key someday.

Miss Lula Mae has a key too, to the house on Calloway that she and Booker paid off in 1984. She showed it to her great-grandchildren at Thanksgiving. "This is the first thing in this family's history that nobody could take away," she told them. "Your great-granddaddy and I made sure of it. Don't you ever let it go cheap."

Aunt Birdie gave away her compass. It was her father's, brass, from his years as a Pullman porter. She gave it to Toya, the girl who came up the porch steps every afternoon for two weeks and finally said she didn't think she was smart. Toya already had the pencil. Now she had the compass too.

"You don't need a map if you know which way is right," Birdie told her.

And Brother Walter reads aloud. Every Thursday afternoon, the children from the block come to his front room, and he reads to them for an hour from whatever book they bring. He does all the voices. He stops at the good parts to ask what they think will happen next. He's teaching them to read, yes. But he's also teaching them to sit still with somebody older and listen. That's the first lesson. It's the same one Birdie taught on her porch.

Everything comes back around.

If you have an elder in your life, they're carrying something they want to give you. It might be a pair of scissors or a notebook or a key. It might be a story they've never told. It might be a sentence you won't understand for twenty years.

Go sit with them. Let them pass it.

And then, when it's your turn, don't keep it.

Pass it.

# Closing

That's the porch, and those are the people on it.

If any of these elders reminded you of somebody, I hope you'll do one thing this week. Call them. Or sit with them. Or if they're gone, sit with somebody who knew them, and ask for a story you've never heard.

Here are three questions that open almost any elder up. Try them. What was the hardest year of your life, and how did you get through it? Who taught you the most, and what did they teach you? What do you want me to remember after you're gone?

Then write down what they say, the way they said it. Use the lined pages at the back of this book. Smart is just noticing, written down.

The people of Calloway Street have a lot more to say. Next time, they're taking the filter off.
