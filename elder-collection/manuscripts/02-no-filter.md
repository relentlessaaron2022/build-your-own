# Back Cover

They raised your mama. They survived things you only read about. They have exactly zero minutes left for your nonsense.

No Filter is a love letter to the elders who say what they mean the first time. Grandma Pearl, who can end a family argument with one raised eyebrow. Uncle Otis, who has defended a thermostat setting for fifty-one years. Granddaddy Booker, who whispers sermon reviews loud enough for the choir to hear. And Deacon Ross, who will pull your pants up in a public parking lot.

Ten laugh-out-loud stories. One hundred hand-painted illustrations. And under every side-eye, a lesson you'll be grateful for later.

Chile, please. Just read it.

# Dedication

For every elder who told us the truth before we were ready to hear it. You were right. Don't say it.

# Introduction

There's a look. You know the one.

It starts in the eyebrows. One goes up, slow, like a curtain at a show nobody bought tickets for. The other one stays exactly where it is. The lips press together. The arms fold. And then nothing happens for a long time, and the nothing is the loudest thing in the room.

If you were raised by Black elders, you have received this look. You may have received it this week. You may be receiving it right now, from somebody who saw the cover of this book and has opinions.

[Relentless: put your own moment here. Whose look was it? What did you do to earn it? Two or three plain sentences.]

This book is about the elders who stopped filtering. Some of them never had a filter to begin with. Others spent fifty years being polite at work, polite at the bank, polite to people who didn't deserve it, and one day, around their seventieth birthday, they just set the whole thing down and walked away from it.

We laugh at them. We should. They're hilarious, and they know it. But listen closely and you'll hear something underneath the sass. Every "chile, please" has a reason. Every side-eye has a history. These are people who learned the hard way that you can waste your life waiting to be liked, and they're done waiting.

The elders of Calloway Street are back. You met some of them on Aunt Birdie's porch. This time, they're not being gentle.

Brace yourself.

# Chapter 1

At the Henderson family reunion in 2023, cousin Bernard stood up during dessert and announced that he was starting a business.

It was his fourth business. Everybody knew about the other three. The car detailing service that detailed exactly one car, his own. The food truck that never got a truck. The cryptocurrency situation, which nobody discussed in front of his mother.

Bernard made his speech. It was a good speech. He'd practiced it. He talked about vision and investors and something called a "scalable platform." When he finished, the pavilion went quiet. Everybody looked at their peach cobbler.

Then everybody looked at Grandma Pearl.

She was sitting at the head of the second picnic table with her arms crossed and one eyebrow raised so high it nearly left her face. She let the silence stretch. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Somebody's baby started to fuss and its mother hushed it.

Then Pearl said, "Bernard. Baby. Who's going to do the work?"

Bernard opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

"Because the last three times," Pearl continued, "you had the vision. You had the speech. Lord knows you had the investors." She glanced at Bernard's mother, who looked at the ground. "What you didn't have was anybody getting up at five in the morning. Including you."

She uncrossed her arms, picked up her fork, and went back to her cobbler. Conversation over.

You could argue she was harsh. A few people at that reunion did, later, in the car. But here's what happened next. Bernard came by Pearl's house the following Saturday. They sat at her kitchen table for three hours. She made him write down, in pencil, every single task the business would need for its first month. Then she made him circle the ones he would personally do before nine in the morning.

He circled two. She made him start over.

Bernard's lawn care business is three years old now. He has two trucks and four employees, and he still gets up at five. He brought Pearl a framed photo of the first truck for Christmas. She hung it in the hallway, right next to her grandchildren's graduation pictures.

That's the thing about elders with no filter. They're not trying to hurt you. They just don't have time to say it twice. Most of them spent decades in rooms where they had to soften everything. They watched people lie politely to each other, and they watched those polite lies cost people years. By the time they got old enough to stop, they'd decided the truth, said once, plainly, was the kindest thing they had left to give.

Miss Odessa calls it "saying what I said." She'll tell you something you don't want to hear, and when you push back, she doesn't argue. She just says, "I said what I said," and lets you sit with it. "I'm not going to dress it up," she says. "You're grown. You can handle it naked."

Pops Earl does it with his glasses. When he's about to tell you something you won't like, he takes them off. Slowly. Folds them. Sets them on the table. By the time they're down, you already know you're in trouble.

If you have one of these elders in your life, here's some advice. When the eyebrow goes up, don't explain. Don't defend. Just listen. It's going to sting. It's also, almost always, going to be right.

And if you're lucky, they'll still be around to see you prove it.

# Chapter 2

Uncle Otis and Grandma Pearl have been married fifty-one years, and for fifty-one years they have fought over the thermostat.

He likes it at sixty-eight. She likes it at seventy-four. There is no middle. They have tried a middle. In 1988, a marriage counselor at the church suggested seventy-one, and both of them were insulted.

The thermostat lives in the hallway between the kitchen and the front room, which makes it neutral territory, which makes it a battlefield. Otis walks past and nudges it down. Pearl walks past and nudges it up. Neither one ever mentions it. It's been going on so long that their grandchildren grew up thinking the hallway was supposed to make a clicking sound.

In 2009, Pearl escalated. She put a piece of masking tape over the thermostat and wrote on it in black marker: DON'T TOUCH. Otis peeled the tape back, moved it to sixty-eight, and stuck the tape back down perfectly. She didn't notice for three days.

When she did notice, she didn't say anything. She just started keeping a sweater on the back of his recliner. A big one. Cable knit. Every time he sat down, there it was. Draped. Waiting. A silent message that said, "You want it cold? Wear this, then."

He wore it. For fourteen years, he's worn it. He says it's because it's comfortable. Everybody knows it's because he refuses to lose.

The thermostat wars aren't really about the temperature. Ask anybody who's been married long enough and they'll tell you. Long marriages need something small to fight about, a safe place to put the friction so it doesn't end up somewhere dangerous. Otis and Pearl have weathered layoffs, a fire, two kids through college and one kid through things they don't talk about. The thermostat gave them somewhere to argue that never mattered.

Pops Earl and Miss Mavis have their own version. Theirs is the volume on the television. Pops says he can't hear it. Mavis says he can hear fine and just likes to make the windows shake. They argue about it every evening at seven, right when the news comes on, and then they sit side by side on the couch and watch it together, at whatever volume won that night.

Pops is famous for one other thing. He falls asleep in his chair at about eight-fifteen every night, head back, mouth open, snoring loud enough to compete with the TV. But try to change the channel. Try it. One eye will open. Just one. And he'll say, perfectly clear, "I was watching that."

"You were asleep," Mavis says.

"I was resting my eyes."

Deacon Ross has the same skill. Sound asleep in his recliner, and the second a grandchild reaches for the remote, he's awake. He claims he can feel it. Nobody's ever proven otherwise.

And then there's the plastic. Every elder house on Calloway Street had a front room with plastic on the furniture at some point. The good sofa. Nobody sat on it. It was for company that never came, or the pastor, or a funeral. Miss Odessa still has hers, and when her great-nephew asked why she bothered, she put her hands on her hips and said, "Because this sofa was paid for in cash in 1979, and it's going to outlive both of us."

It probably will.

# Chapter 3

Deacon Ross once pulled a grown man's pants up in the parking lot of the Piggly Wiggly.

The grown man was his grandson Terrell, who was nineteen at the time and very proud of his outfit. Terrell's jeans sat somewhere around the middle of his thighs. His boxers were on display for most of Fifth Street. He was carrying two bags of groceries for his grandfather and could not defend himself.

Deacon Ross set his own bag down on the hood of the Buick. He walked around behind Terrell. He took a firm grip on both belt loops. And in one smooth motion, like a man who'd been waiting years for the chance, he hoisted.

Terrell's feet left the ground for half a second. The woman loading her trunk two spaces over dropped her keys.

"There," said Deacon Ross. "Now you look like somebody's child."

Terrell still tells that story. He's thirty-one now, an attorney, and he says it was the moment he understood his grandfather. "I thought he was embarrassing me," Terrell says. "Turns out he was scared for me."

That's the part the younger generation sometimes misses. When elders comment on how you dress, they're almost never talking about fashion. They grew up in a world where a young Black man with his pants low could be stopped, searched, or worse. Where a young Black woman in the wrong outfit could be dismissed before she ever opened her mouth. Their whole lives, they dressed to survive. Sunday best wasn't vanity. It was armor.

So when Aunt Birdie looks at your ripped jeans and says, "Did you get attacked on the way over?" she's being funny. She's also asking a real question. She wants to know if you understand how the world sees you. She's hoping you do. She's terrified you don't.

Pops Earl has a different method. He doesn't say anything at all. He just looks. Starts at your shoes and moves up, real slow, and by the time he gets to your face, you've already gone back upstairs to change.

Mr. Jasper, who has worn the same army field jacket covered in pins for forty years, is the one elder on Calloway who has no ground to stand on, and he knows it. He'll look at a grandchild's outfit and say, "I respect it. I don't understand it, but I respect it." Then he'll tell you the story behind every pin on his jacket, and you'll be there for an hour.

Miss Odessa does the full inspection. Hat to heels. She'll fix your collar, straighten your earring, and pull a lint roller out of her handbag like a magician pulling a rabbit. Nobody knows how many lint rollers that handbag holds.

Granddaddy Booker doesn't care what you're wearing. He's ninety next year and he's past it. What he cares about is your face. Specifically your cheeks. He pinches them. Every time. He did it when you were three, and he'll do it when you're forty-three, standing in front of your coworkers at his birthday party. "I can't help it," he says. "I remember when that whole face fit in my hand."

You can roll your eyes. Pops Earl certainly does, when his own grandkids try to tell him something's "in style." His eyes roll so far back you can see the whites. "In style," he repeats, like it's a disease. "In style. I had bell-bottoms. I know all about in style."

But next time an elder comments on your clothes, try hearing what's underneath it. "Pull your pants up" usually means "I need you to come home tonight." "Is that what you're wearing?" usually means "I want them to see what I see when I look at you."

Then go change. Or don't. You're grown.

Just know they're only saying it because they love you.

# Chapter 4

Mr. Jasper got his first smartphone in 2021. He has read every text message since then at arm's length.

He holds the phone straight out in front of him, as far away as his arm will go, and tilts his head back, and squints through the bottom of his glasses. When that doesn't work, he moves it closer. Then farther. Then he hands it to whoever's nearest and says, "What's this say?"

His grandson set the font size to the largest possible setting. Jasper's phone now displays about four words at a time. A single text from his daughter takes nine screens. He reads every one of them, out loud, slowly, with commentary.

Coach Clarence has the opposite problem. He texts too much. He's in a group chat with his old summer league players, now men in their forties and fifties, and he responds to every single message. Every one. Photos of grandbabies, links to articles, jokes. And every response is the same. "Ok."

Not "okay." Not "OK!" Just "Ok." With a period. Followed by a new line. "Love, Coach."

The men have tried explaining that you don't have to sign a text message. Clarence refuses. "My name goes on what I say," he says. "That's how I was raised."

Then there's the other side of the phone situation. The grandchildren's phones. The elders of Calloway Street have opinions.

Miss Odessa invited her granddaughter over for Sunday dinner. The granddaughter spent the meal scrolling. Odessa didn't say a word. She just turned her chair a quarter turn, so she was facing the wall instead of the girl, and ate the rest of her greens in silence. It took the granddaughter ten full minutes to notice. When she finally looked up, Odessa said, without turning around, "Oh, you're here now? I thought you'd left."

Miss Vivian does it with a look over the top of her teal glasses, a look that has ended sixty years of conversations.

Grandma Pearl is more direct. Her grandson was on his phone at the kitchen table, so she took it out of his hand and held it up over her head. She's five foot one. He's six foot three. He could have taken it back easily. He didn't dare. She held it up there for a full minute, both of them looking at it, until he said, "Yes ma'am, I'm sorry." Pops Earl did the same thing at Thanksgiving. The whole table cheered.

And Uncle Otis has mastered the art of selective hearing. Tell him dinner's ready, he's at the table before you finish the sentence. Tell him the trash needs to go out, he cups his hand to his ear and says, "Hmm? What's that?" He can hear a dropped quarter from two rooms away. He has not heard "take out the trash" since 1987.

Under all the jokes, the elders are saying something serious about attention. They grew up when the only way to be with someone was to actually be with them. No screens. No scrolling. If you were at the table, you were at the table. They know how short life is. They've buried enough people to know. When you're on your phone in front of them, they're not mad about the phone. They're doing math. How many more Sunday dinners? How many more Thanksgivings? And you're spending this one looking at a stranger's vacation pictures.

So put it face down. Turn it over. Better yet, leave it in the car.

And when your elder holds their own phone out at arm's length and asks you, "What's this say?" take a minute. Read it to them. Slowly. It's probably from somebody who loves them.

# Chapter 5

Granddaddy Booker reviews the sermon while it's still being preached.

He sits in the fourth pew on the left at Greater Hope Missionary Baptist, the same seat since 1964. Mother Gloria sits beside him. They've been church friends for sixty years, and for sixty years they've whispered their way through every service.

Their whispering is not quiet. It's the kind of whisper that carries. Booker's voice, in particular, was built for calling cows. When the visiting preacher went long last Easter, the whole front half of the church heard Booker lean over and say, "He's circling the airport. Somebody give him a runway."

The choir heard it too. Two of the altos had to turn around.

Mother Gloria is better at it. Her technique is the fan. She has a wooden-handled church fan from a funeral home that closed in 1983, and she uses it to cover her mouth while she talks. To everybody else, she looks like a woman keeping cool. In reality, she's giving Booker a running commentary on who's wearing what, who came in late, and whose son is sitting with a woman who is not his wife.

Miss Mavis fans too, but she uses hers to communicate. A slow fan means she agrees with the preacher. A fast fan means he needs to wrap it up. A fan that stops completely, mid-stroke, means something has been said from the pulpit that she will be discussing at length in the parking lot.

Deacon Ross and Miss Odessa have their own system. They don't whisper at all. They just exchange looks. A side-eye from Odessa, a tiny nod from the Deacon, and an entire conversation has happened. Their grandchildren swear they've seen these two discuss a whole church budget without moving their lips.

There are peppermints, too. Every elder church lady carries peppermints in her purse. The soft kind and the hard kind, wrapped in crinkly plastic, and she will unwrap one during the quietest moment of the prayer, as slowly as humanly possible, so the crinkle lasts for thirty seconds. Then she'll hand one to the child next to her, who has been squirming, and the child will go still and grateful.

Then there's "Bless your heart." Every Black church elder can say it in at least four ways. There's the sincere version, warm and slow, which means "I'm sorry you're going through that." There's the quick version, which means "That's a shame." There's the version with a pause before it, which means "You did this to yourself." And there's the version with the eyebrow, which means something no preacher would approve of.

Underneath all the commentary is something more serious. Church was the one place where these elders could be fully themselves, all their lives. At work they were careful. In town they were watched. But on Sunday morning, in their own pews, in their own building, among their own people, they could laugh and judge and gossip and sing at the top of their lungs. The whispering isn't disrespect. It's ownership. That's their church. They paid for those pews. Some of them helped build the walls.

And when the sermon is truly good, when the preacher lands it, you'll see Booker stop whispering. He'll sit forward. He'll say "Well," low, the way old men in church say it, which means "Yes. That. Go on."

That's the highest review a sermon can get.

# Chapter 6

At Uncle Otis's house, spades is not a game. It's a reckoning.

Every Friday night, the card table goes up in his front room. Same table, same chairs, same deck of cards so soft from use you could fold them like cloth. Otis partners with Miss Lula Mae, and they've been partners since 1981. In forty-five years of Friday nights, they've developed a set of signals so complicated nobody else has ever cracked it. A cough. A scratch of the ear. A particular way Lula Mae sets down her sweet tea. Everybody knows they're signaling. Nobody can prove it.

"We don't cheat," Lula Mae says. "We communicate."

Pops Earl and Grandma Pearl play against them most weeks, and Pops is the worst loser on Calloway Street. When he loses a hand, he doesn't say anything. He just gathers the cards very, very slowly, and squares the deck against the table, tap, tap, tap, for a full minute, while everybody waits.

Then there's the trash talk.

Spades trash talk is an art form, and the elders are masters. Miss Vivian, partnered with Coach Clarence on alternate Fridays, is the best of them. She slaps down the winning card and says, "Read it and weep. Then read it again, because you didn't understand it the first time." When Clarence reneges, which happens about once a month, she doesn't even look at him. She just says, "I'm going to pray for you, Clarence," in a tone that suggests the prayers will not be kind.

Mother Gloria doesn't play anymore. She watches. Her job, self-appointed, is to keep track of every mistake every player makes, and to list them out loud, on her fingers, after the game. "One, you led with the wrong suit. Two, you didn't count the spades. Three, you looked at your partner like you wanted a divorce."

Deacon Ross sits in the corner and laughs. He laughs at his own jokes, mainly. He tells the same five every Friday and laughs harder every year. "What did the deacon say when the offering was short? Let us pray!" He slaps his knee. Nobody else laughs. He doesn't need them to.

Why do the elders take spades so seriously?

Ask Uncle Otis and he'll tell you. "Spades is a family game. You can't win alone. You need a partner who knows how you think." Card nights were where a lot of the elders of Calloway Street learned to trust each other. In the years when Black folks couldn't go to a lot of places, they went to each other's houses. The card table was the club, the bar, the lounge, the therapist's office. People got engaged at that table. People got bad news at that table. People found out who would show up for them.

So when your elders trash-talk you at cards, take it as an honor. They only do it to people they love. Strangers get politeness. Family gets "Who taught you to play? I need to know so I can call them."

And if you ever get invited to Otis's table on a Friday night, there's only one rule. Never, ever renege.

They will remember.

# Chapter 7

Mr. Jasper has been early to everything for seventy-eight years.

Church. Doctor's appointments. Weddings, funerals, cookouts. He arrives fifteen minutes before whatever time you told him, and then he stands there, looking at his wristwatch, tapping his foot, until you show up. Then he says the same thing every time. "There you are."

Not "hello." Not "good to see you." "There you are." Like you've been lost.

Coach Clarence is the same, but louder. Forty years of coaching taught him that "on time is late." When his grandkids pick him up for dinner, he's already waiting on the porch in his jacket, keys in hand, and he checks his watch as they pull up even if they're early. "Practice starts when I say it starts," he says, though nobody has been to practice in decades.

But here's the strange part. These same elders who can't stand you being late will not, under any circumstances, be rushed themselves.

Watch Coach Clarence walk to the car. He's in no hurry. He's leaning on his cane, smirking a little, taking one step at a time. You're holding the door. It's raining. He's taking his time. "I'll get there," he says. "Didn't I always get there?"

Deacon Ross has the same cane and the same smirk. Ask him to hurry and he'll stop entirely, just to make the point. "I've been rushing my whole life," he says. "I rushed to work. I rushed home. I rushed to church and rushed to pay bills. I'm done. I'm not going back."

Miss Mavis and Aunt Birdie do it with tea. You come over in a hurry, you've got somewhere to be, you just need to drop something off. They'll pour you a cup anyway. And they'll sip theirs, pinky out, completely unbothered, while you stand there jiggling your keys. "Sit down," Mavis says. "Whatever it is will still be there in ten minutes."

It usually is.

There's a reason for both sides of this. The elders were early their whole lives because being late was dangerous. Late to the job meant fired. Late to the bus meant walking. Late to the appointment meant they gave your spot to someone else, someone white, and nobody apologized. So they learned to be early. It was protection.

But they also learned what hurry costs. They watched people rush their whole lives toward something and die before they got there. They watched friends work themselves into the ground. And somewhere around retirement, a lot of them made a decision. The world rushed them for sixty years. The world can wait now.

Brother Walter sums it up from his wheelchair, legs crossed at the ankle, a tight little grin on his face. "I'm going to sit right here. You go on ahead if you need to. I'll catch up. I always do."

And then there's the alarm clock. Every elder on Calloway Street is awake by five in the morning, whether they need to be or not. They don't have jobs. They don't have appointments. They're just up. Making coffee, reading the paper, calling you at 6:15 to ask why you're not up yet.

"The early part of the day belongs to you," Aunt Birdie says. "Everything after nine belongs to everybody else."

Maybe that's the real lesson. Be early to the things that matter. And never, ever hurry the things that matter more.

# Chapter 8

Last spring, a young man at the senior center on Twelfth Street gave a presentation on the history of the civil rights movement.

He was a graduate student. He was very well prepared. He had slides. He talked for forty minutes about the marches and the sit-ins and the boycotts. He used words like "nonviolent direct action" and "organizing infrastructure."

Grandma Pearl sat in the front row in her big round red glasses and listened politely the whole time. When he finished and asked if there were any questions, she raised her hand.

"Baby," she said, "that was nice. But I was there."

The room went quiet. The young man went a little pale.

"You said the marchers were afraid," Pearl said. "We weren't afraid. We were terrified. There's a difference. Afraid, you can talk yourself out of. Terrified, you just have to walk through. And you said it was organized. It was organized the way a kitchen is organized when twelve people are cooking Thanksgiving dinner. Everybody was yelling."

Then she told him about the day in 1960 when she was twenty and sat at a downtown lunch counter for six hours. About the ketchup somebody poured on her head. About walking home with it drying in her hair and her mother washing it out in the kitchen sink without saying a word, crying the whole time.

The graduate student took notes for an hour. He came back the next week with a tape recorder. Pearl has done four interviews with him since. He's writing a book. She's in the acknowledgments.

That's the thing about elders. Some of what you're learning in school, they lived. They're not being rude when they correct you. They're being accurate.

Brother Walter gets this all the time. Young people explain things to him that he watched happen. He just puts on his sunglasses, indoors, and leans back and lets them finish. Then he says, "Mm-hm. Now let me tell you what actually happened."

The elders also keep receipts, literally. Mr. Jasper has a shoebox of every receipt from every major purchase he's made since 1972. When the furniture store tried to charge him twice in 1994, he had proof. When the bank said he'd missed a payment in 2003, he had the canceled check. He'll sit with you and count off, on his fingers, every time somebody tried to cheat him. There are a lot of fingers.

"We learned to keep paper," he says, "because they never believed us without it."

And at night, they watch the news and yell at it. All of them. Three elders on a couch, shouting at the television like it can hear them. They yell at the politicians. They yell at the reporters. They yell when somebody says "unprecedented," because nearly everything that happens has happened before, and they were there for it the first time.

"Too old for lies," Miss Vivian says. "I've heard every lie there is. You'll have to come up with a new one if you want to get past me."

There's an edge in how they say all of this. Sometimes it's funny. Sometimes it isn't. They lived through things that got written down wrong, or not written down at all. They've watched their own history get explained back to them by people who weren't born yet.

So when your elder says, "I was there," stop talking. Get the tape recorder.

# Chapter 9

On Tuesday and Thursday afternoons from one to two, Miss Lula Mae is not available.

She's watching her stories. She's watched the same soap opera since 1968. Many of the original characters have died, been replaced, come back from the dead, and died again. Lula Mae has seen all of it. During that hour, she hangs a sign on her chest, handwritten on a piece of cardboard with a string through it. DO NOT DISTURB.

She means it. Granddaddy Booker once tried to ask her where the checkbook was during a particularly dramatic wedding episode. She didn't answer. She didn't look at him. She just raised one finger, slowly, and pointed at the sign.

He found the checkbook himself.

Aunt Hattie has a sign too, for a different reason. Hers says the same thing, but she wears it on Sunday afternoons, when she does her crossword. Her husband, Mr. Jasper, is the only one allowed to interrupt, and only to suggest a word. If he's wrong, she doesn't use it. If he's right, she doesn't tell him.

The elders of Calloway Street have very clear boundaries. They spent their whole lives being available. To jobs, to children, to churches, to whoever needed something. Now they've finally drawn some lines, and they don't care if you like where the lines are.

Miss Mavis's line is the hand. You start telling her something she doesn't want to hear, gossip especially, and up it comes, palm out, right in front of your face. She doesn't say a word. The hand says everything. Aunt Birdie does it too, but softer, and with a little smile, like she's sorry. She's not sorry.

Miss Odessa has the most polite method, which is also the most dangerous. When a nosy neighbor asks her something personal, she smiles. A big, bright, beautiful smile, teeth together, eyes totally flat. And she says, "Oh, now, why would you want to know that?" And she just keeps smiling until the neighbor goes away.

Mr. Reggie's boundary is his plate. You can have anything in his house except what's on his plate. He'll cook for you. He'll fix you a plate of your own. He'll send you home with leftovers. But if you reach for a piece of chicken off his plate, he'll pull it back to his chest like a quarterback protecting the ball. "Get your own," he says. "I'm sharing everything else."

Here's what's funny. These same elders who are so strict about minding their business are absolutely in yours. You'll see them at the salon on Saturday, four of them under the hood dryers, getting every detail of every family on Calloway Street. Who's pregnant. Who got fired. Whose son is back in town. They want to know everything about you. They'd just rather you didn't know anything about them.

That's not hypocrisy. Or, all right, it's a little bit hypocrisy. But it comes from somewhere real. A lot of elders grew up with nothing private. Small houses. Big families. Thin walls. Landlords who walked in without knocking. Bosses who asked questions they had no right to ask. Privacy was a luxury, and now that they've got it, they're not giving it up.

And they keep up with everyone else's business because, frankly, somebody has to. That's how a community looks out for itself. The salon gossip is also the early warning system. They know who's sick before the family does. They know whose lights got cut off. They know who needs a casserole.

So respect the sign. Respect the hand. Don't reach for the chicken.

And don't be surprised when Miss Odessa already knows what you did last weekend. She was under the dryer when your cousin told it.

# Chapter 10

Here's a secret about the elders of Calloway Street. All that sass is love.

It just comes with an edge.

Mother Gloria and Mr. Reggie, who've known each other for sixty years, can't be in the same room for five minutes without insulting each other. She says his suits are too loud. He says her hats are a fire hazard. And then one of them says something about 1966, and they both start laughing, and they have to hold on to each other to stay upright.

Miss Mavis and Coach Clarence do the same thing. So do Miss Odessa and Pops Earl. They tease each other relentlessly. And when Clarence's wife passed, Mavis was at his door every morning for a month with breakfast. When Pops had his knee surgery, Odessa drove him to every single physical therapy appointment, and complained about his driving directions the whole way.

That's how it works. They roast you because they love you. The insults are the proof. Strangers get politeness. Family gets the truth.

Mr. Reggie and Miss Vivian have been married thirty years, and every time it rains, they share one umbrella and argue the whole way. She says he holds it too high. He says she walks too fast. They've had umbrellas for both of them for thirty years. They still only use one.

Granddaddy Booker and Miss Lula Mae do it too, but slower. Booker holds the umbrella. Lula Mae holds his arm and her cane. They argue about which way to walk. They always end up going her way.

Every elder in this book has the same habit. They'll never say "I love you" softly. Instead, they'll say, "You look skinny. Take a plate." They'll say, "Call your mother." They'll say, "Who told you that you could wear that?" and then they'll fix your collar, and their hands will linger there for just a second.

Even the candy dish is love. That glass dish on the coffee table, full of butterscotch that's been there since 1993? You'll hear "Don't touch that, that's for company" a hundred times. Then one day, when you're sad, somebody will hand you one without a word. The butterscotch is always for you, in the end.

The collard greens are love. A pot so big you could bathe in it, cooked for six hours, and then they'll tell you they don't know why they made so much. They knew exactly why. They knew you were coming.

At the end of every family reunion, every holiday, every Sunday dinner, there's a photo. And if you look at those photos carefully, you'll notice something. The grandchildren are making faces. The middle generation is checking their phones. And the elders, in the front row, are the only ones smiling.

They know something the rest of us don't. They know these days are numbered. They know the edge they show you is armor, and the love underneath it is soft enough to break.

So the next time your elder roasts you, side-eyes you, calls you out in front of everybody, try saying "thank you."

They'll say, "For what? Chile, please."

But they'll be smiling.

# Closing

If you laughed, good. They'd want you to.

If you saw your grandmother in Grandma Pearl, or your granddaddy in Deacon Ross, or yourself in cousin Bernard, even better. That means you were raised by somebody who loved you enough to tell you the truth.

Here's your assignment, and you can't get out of it. Go see the elder with the eyebrow. Bring something: food, flowers, a good card. Let them roast you. Ask them the question you've been afraid to ask. Then sit there while they answer, no matter how long it takes, and don't look at your phone once.

Write down what they say, word for word, especially the parts that sting. There are lined pages at the back of this book for exactly that.

The people of Calloway Street are just getting started. Next time, they're getting up off the porch. They've got places to go.
