# T04 — Catalogue Spin-Off Proposal (Relentless Aaron)

The feeling we want first is recognition. A reader who once kept a secret for someone powerful, or paid for somebody else's choices, should open this book and feel caught. Right now that reader is stuck between the life they owed and the life they want. By the last page they should believe a person can walk out of debt that was never theirs. Everything below is built to close that gap.

## Logline

For eleven years Odessa "Dess" Marlowe kept the numbers for the most feared organization in Harlem, and she did six years in a women's facility upstate rather than give up a single page. Now she is home, the Old Man is gone, and every crew fighting for his seat believes she is carrying the only copy of the ledger in her head. They are right. The question that eats at her is not whether she will survive. It is whether she can stop being the woman who remembers everything, long enough to become someone she would actually want to be.

## Why Now

**The reader is ready for the quiet ones.** Street lit has given readers the shooter, the boss, the hustler, the wife. It has rarely given them the person in the back room with the calculator, the one who sees all of it and touches none of it, and pays anyway. That angle feels new without leaving the genre. It keeps the power plays and the prison yard. It adds a mind under pressure, and that is where the redemption lives.

**The catalogue has a door open.** A title like Last Kingpin carries a built-in question: what happens after the last one? LEDGER answers it from the ground floor. A reader who finished the earlier book gets a fresh story in a world they already trust. A new reader needs no homework. That makes it a clean entry point into the whole Relentless Aaron shelf, including Push, the Sugar Daddy universe and Extra Marital Affairs, which can all be cross-promoted at the end of each installment.

**It serializes naturally.** Dess has a list of names in her head. Each name is a debt, a danger, or a door. That structure gives us cliffhangers built into the spine of the book, which fits a weekly drop schedule on social and on a direct-to-reader storefront.

**It feeds the 90-day revenue target.** A serialized release creates repeat purchase moments instead of one launch day. It gives the social team short, quotable lines every week. It gives the music side a theme to score. CoCo Kity can voice a short audio teaser in the "Street Stories" format, and an Atlanta Music Alliance artist can carry a single tied to the book's mood. The circle gets lifted with the book, not after it. [VERIFY: CoCo Kity and Atlanta Music Alliance participation confirmed before any announcement]

**The theme hits home right now.** Plenty of readers are carrying something they did not choose: a family debt, a parent's record, a partner's mess, a job that asked them to look away. Dess is the patron saint of looking away, and the book is about the day she stops. That is the hook for the back cover, the ads and the first post.

## Chapter Breakdown

Twenty-two chapters, built in four movements so each serialized drop lands on a turn.

**Movement One: Release**

1. **Gate.** Dess walks out of the facility with a clear plastic bag and a head full of numbers she has never written down. Nobody she expects is waiting. Somebody she doesn't expect is.
2. **The Room on 141st.** Her sister's apartment, a fold-out couch, and a niece who looks at her like a stranger. Dess counts the cracks in the ceiling to fall asleep, the way she did inside.
3. **Parole Officer.** A tired man named Halloran gives her the rules. She notices he is wearing a watch worth more than his salary, and files it away without meaning to.
4. **First Visitor.** Teddy Reyes, once the Old Man's driver, now running his own small crew, offers her a job and a warning in the same breath. She turns down both.
5. **The Weight.** Alone in a laundromat at 2 a.m., Dess recites the ledger silently to keep it from slipping, and realizes she is afraid of forgetting more than she is afraid of dying.

**Movement Two: The Seat**

6. **Three Crowns.** We meet the three factions moving for the empty seat: Teddy's crew from the north end, the Pryce brothers out of the projects on Lenox, and a polished newcomer called Mr. Sayles who never raises his voice.
7. **Halloran's Price.** The parole officer turns out to be on someone's payroll. He wants page one. Dess learns that even her freedom has a balance due.
8. **Niece.** Kiana, sixteen, gets pulled toward the Pryce brothers' corner. Dess sees her own beginning in the girl's sneakers and her loud laugh.
9. **The Ledger Speaks.** A flashback to the night the Old Man first trusted her with the books, and the first name she ever wrote down: a boy who later disappeared.
10. **Job at the Bodega.** Honest work stocking shelves for a Dominican grocer, Mr. Almonte. For two days she feels normal. On the third day, someone leaves an envelope in the register with her name on it.
11. **Sayles.** A meeting in a quiet restaurant. Sayles knows things about her that only the ledger should know. That means someone else has been talking.

**Movement Three: Fracture**

12. **The Other Copy.** Dess discovers there was a second keeper: a woman she trained, Marisol, now missing. If Marisol is alive, Dess is no longer the only key, and no longer worth protecting.
13. **Yard Memory.** Flashback to prison: the fight in the yard where Dess learned that silence is a weapon and a cage at the same time.
14. **Kiana's First Run.** The niece carries a package for the Pryce brothers. Dess follows her through Harlem at night, and for the first time in years she has to decide whether to step into the street instead of keeping score of it.
15. **Teddy's Confession.** The old driver tells her what really happened the night the Old Man fell. It changes the meaning of every number she has been guarding. [VERIFY: continuity with prior book on how the kingpin fell]
16. **The Sister.** Her sister, Renee, finally says what she has held for six years: that Dess chose the Old Man over her own family, and the family paid the bill.
17. **Marisol.** Dess finds her, alive, scared, and holding a written copy. Two women, one secret, and only one way out that doesn't end in a grave.

**Movement Four: Balance**

18. **Halloran Pushes.** The parole officer threatens to violate her and send her back upstate unless she delivers the ledger by Friday.
19. **The Trade.** Dess sets the three crowns against each other with selected truths from the ledger, giving each just enough to start a war that keeps them off Kiana.
20. **Burn.** On the roof of her sister's building, Dess and Marisol burn the written copy page by page. Dess lets herself forget, one name at a time. It feels like bleeding.
21. **Friday.** Halloran comes to collect and finds that the only number Dess still remembers is his. The power shifts in a single quiet sentence.
22. **Open Book.** Months later, Dess teaches a bookkeeping class at a community center on Frederick Douglass Boulevard. Kiana sits in the back row. Someone new is waiting outside, and the last line opens the door to book two.

## Sample Opening

**Chapter 1: Gate**

They give you back your own clothes on the way out, and that is the first cruelty.

The jeans still fit, more or less. The blouse does not. Six years ago it was cream colored and it fit across my shoulders like it knew me. Now it has gone the yellow of old teeth, and it hangs. I stand in the release room with a CO watching and I button it all the way to the throat anyway, because the woman who bought this blouse would have buttoned it, and I need her for the next hour. I need somebody to walk out of here who is not me.

The CO slides a plastic bag across the counter. Clear plastic, so they can see you are not taking anything they didn't give you. Inside: a wallet with an expired ID, forty-one dollars, a lipstick I don't remember buying, and a set of keys to an apartment that has not been mine for a long time. No phone. Phones were not a thing they held for you. Phones were evidence.

"Marlowe," she says. "Sign."

I sign. My hand is steady. My hand has always been steady. That was the whole job.

Out in the hallway the floor is waxed so bright it hurts. My sneakers squeak. Somewhere behind me, a gate opens and closes with that deep iron sound you stop hearing after the first year and then start hearing again the last month, when your body knows you are leaving before your mind will let you believe it. I count the steps to the second door. Thirty-eight. I counted them on the way in, too. It was forty-one then. I have gotten taller, or the building has gotten smaller, or I am walking like somebody who wants to get there.

Numbers. That is what I have. That is what I always had.

People think a ledger is a book. It isn't. A book is just where you put it so other people can see. The ledger is the knowing. Who owes. Who paid. Who paid with money and who paid with something you don't write in pencil. For eleven years I knew all of it for a man everybody in Harlem called the Old Man, as if he had no name, as if he had been born gray and dangerous. I never wrote the important parts down. Not once. I kept them the way my grandmother kept scripture, in rows, in order, said under my breath at night so they would not drift.

They asked me about it for six years. Lawyers. Detectives. A woman from the federal side with very kind eyes and very cold hands. They asked nice and they asked ugly. They put a cousin of mine in the next interview room so I could hear him crying. I gave them my name and my date of birth. I gave them nothing else.

What nobody asks is what that silence costs the one who keeps it. It is not one big price. It is small. It is every day. It is lying on a cot at three in the morning with a girl two bunks down screaming for her mother, and you are not thinking about your mother. You are thinking: Row nine. Reyes. Paid in full, April. Partial in June. You are saying it to yourself so you won't lose it, and you hate that you are afraid of losing it. You hate that it's the thing you hold onto in the dark instead of your sister's face, instead of your niece, who was ten when I went in and who wrote me four letters the first year and then none.

I know exactly how many letters. Of course I do.

The last door is glass with wire inside it. Through it I can see a parking lot, a chain-link fence, a strip of brown November grass, and a sky so wide it looks fake. The CO at the desk buzzes it. The lock clicks. For a second I don't move, and the CO looks up, and I can tell she has seen this before: the woman who gets to the door and can't walk through it.

I walk through it.

Cold. That is the first thing. Real cold, not the cold of a cell block where the heat is broken. Wind with somewhere to go. It smells like gasoline and wet leaves and something burning far off, maybe trash, maybe a wood stove in one of those little towns along the road. It goes all the way down into me. I put my hand on my chest like a church lady, and I just breathe it, and I am ashamed of how good it feels. Six years, and the thing that almost breaks me is air.

I look for my sister's car.

Renee drives a blue Honda, or she did. She said in her last call, two months back, that she would try. That was the word she used. Try. My sister has never been a liar. She is something worse for me. She is honest, and she makes me look at what I did.

There is no blue Honda.

There is a van from the county that will take you to the bus station. There is a man in a city sanitation jacket smoking by the fence, waiting for somebody who isn't me. And at the far end of the lot, engine running, there is a black car with tinted windows, so clean the clouds move across the hood.

I know that car. I don't know whose it is now, but I know what it is. It is the kind of car that used to idle outside the Old Man's place on 141st while I sat at the kitchen table and listened to men explain why they were short.

My body does something before I can stop it. My shoulders come up. My eyes go to the exits. My hand closes on the plastic bag, and the forty-one dollars, and I am already doing the math. How far to the road. How fast a woman my age can run in sneakers a size too big. Whether they would do it here, in front of the facility, in daylight. No. They would not. Not here. Which means they want to talk. Which means they want the thing in my head.

Which means they think I still have it.

And here is the part I have never said to anyone, the part I am only saying now because I am standing in the cold with nothing: I do. Every row. Every name. I can feel it in me like a second spine. I could walk over to that car right now and open my mouth and make somebody very rich and a lot of somebodies dead.

The back window rolls down an inch. Just an inch. Enough to say: we see you.

I think about my niece. Kiana. Sixteen now. I think about the four letters. I think about the way Renee said try.

Then I turn my back on that car, and I walk to the county van, and I climb in next to a woman with a cast on her arm who is crying without making any sound. I sit down. I buckle the seat belt, which nobody does. I fold my hands on the plastic bag.

The van pulls out. In the side mirror the black car does not move. It just sits there, patient as interest.

I close my eyes, and I hear myself start, soft, under the engine noise, before I can stop it.

Row one.
