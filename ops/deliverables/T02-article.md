# One Lead, Many Hands

*By DeWitt Gilmore*

You know the feeling. It's late. The house is quiet except for the refrigerator humming and your own thoughts, which are loud. You have a book half-finished, a post you promised yourself you'd put up three days ago, an email you keep opening and closing, and an idea for something bigger that you haven't told anybody about because you don't want to hear yourself say it out loud and not do it.

You are tired, but it's not the kind of tired sleep fixes. It's the tired that comes from carrying everything yourself and still feeling like nothing is moving.

I want to sit with you in that feeling for a minute, because I know it. And then I want to show you a way out of it. Not a trick. A way of working. I call it **One Lead, Many Hands.**

Here's where you are: doing everything, finishing little, and doubting yourself because of it. Here's where you want to be: one clear direction, work getting done in pieces every day, and the quiet confidence that comes from knowing what's finished is really finished.

The distance between those two places is shorter than you think. It runs on four things. A clear goal. One written plan. Small jobs handed out. And nothing called done until it's checked.

### The goal comes first, and it has to be said plainly

Most people who say they have a goal really have a wish. "I want my books to do better." "I want to grow my following." "I want to make a living from this." Those are real desires, and I don't take them lightly. But you can't work toward a fog.

A goal you can run a business on has a number and a clock on it. It tells you what you're building and by when. When I sit down to plan, I write the goal at the top of the page before I write anything else, and I write it like a sentence someone could check me on later. When the goal is plain, a lot of noise goes away on its own. You stop saying yes to everything. You start asking one question about every idea, every invitation, every late-night spark: *does this move me toward the number on the page, or doesn't it?* If it doesn't, it waits. Not forever. Just until the main thing is handled.

That's the first piece of discipline. Not working harder. Choosing what you're working on.

### One written plan, not five in your head

Here's something I've noticed in almost every creative person I've met, myself included. We keep plans the way some people keep cash: folded up in different pockets, a little here, a little there, never counted all at once. There's a plan for the next book in a notebook. A plan for social media in your phone. A plan for the music in a voice memo. A plan for the business in a conversation you had with a friend six months ago.

Five plans is no plan. Five plans means you spend your mornings deciding which plan you're on.

So write one. One page, one file, one place. Everything you're doing goes there, and if it's not there, you're not doing it yet. Each line on that plan is a task with a name, an owner, and a status. "Not started." "In progress." "Done." That's it. Nothing fancy.

There's a reason this works, and it has less to do with business than with the mind. When the plan lives in one place, your head is free to do the work instead of guarding the list. You stop waking up at three in the morning trying to remember what you forgot.

People who have lived under someone else's schedule know something the rest of the world forgets. Structure can be a cage when somebody else builds it. When you build it yourself, it becomes the thing that holds you up. Same walls. Different owner.

### Small jobs, handed out

Now we get to the part most people skip, and it's the part that changes everything.

You are one person. You have one pair of hands and a limited number of good hours in a day. If every task on your plan needs *you*, then your business can only ever be as big as you are on your best day. And you don't have a best day every day. Nobody does.

The answer is to break the work into small jobs and hand them out.

Small is the key word. "Grow the brand" is not a job. Nobody can pick that up and finish it by Friday. "Write three captions for the new book cover, under forty words each, in the same voice as the last post" is a job. Someone can do that. Someone can finish that. And you can look at it afterward and know whether it's right.

When I hand out a job, I try to put four things on it:

1. **What it is.** One sentence, plain.
2. **What finished looks like.** The length, the format, where it goes.
3. **What to stay away from.** Words we don't use, lines we don't cross, facts we don't make up.
4. **Who checks it.** Because somebody has to.

Those hands can be people. An editor, a cover designer, a friend who's good with video, someone in your circle who knows the music side better than you. They can also be tools. These days some of the hands I work with are software, and I give them a job sheet the same way I'd give it to a person. The rules don't change. Clear job, clear finish line, clear check.

And here's what nobody tells you about handing things out: it's humbling. You have to admit you can't do it all. You have to trust somebody else's hands with something you care about. For a lot of us, that's harder than the work itself.

The lead doesn't do every job. The lead makes sure every job has a name on it and that the names add up to the goal.

### Nothing is done until it's checked

This is the rule that makes the whole thing honest.

You've been here before. Someone tells you a thing is handled. You move on. Two weeks later you find out it was "mostly" handled, or handled in a way that has to be redone, or never handled at all. Now you've lost the two weeks and the trust.

So we don't call anything done because somebody says so. Not even me. A job is done when it's been checked against what finished was supposed to look like. Is it the right length? Is it in the right place? Does it sound like us? Are the facts true? Did it stay away from what it was told to stay away from?

If yes, it's done. If not, it goes back, and nobody takes it personally. Going back is not failure. Going back is the system working.

I'll be honest with you about why this matters to me so much. When you write under your own name, and when people trust that name, every sentence that goes out is a promise. I don't put out stories about my life that didn't happen. I don't put out numbers I can't stand behind. If I'm not sure, it gets marked and checked before it goes anywhere. That's not caution for its own sake. That's respect for the reader.

Checking is also how you sleep at night. When you know everything marked "done" really is, you stop carrying the low hum of worry about what might be wrong out there with your name on it.

### When you have to start over

Some of you reading this aren't building from a running start. You're building from zero. Maybe from below zero. Maybe you lost a business, a marriage, a job, years. Maybe you're coming home from somewhere and the world kept moving without you.

I want to speak to you directly, because this way of working was made for starting over.

When you're rebuilding, the hardest part isn't the work. It's the size of it. You look at everything you need to do and it feels like trying to drink a river. So you freeze. Or you do a little of everything and finish none of it, and then you blame yourself, and the blame makes it harder to start tomorrow.

One Lead, Many Hands breaks the river into cups.

Write the goal. One sentence. Write the plan. One page. Pick the smallest job on it, the one you can finish today, and finish it. Check it. Mark it done. Tomorrow, do it again. If someone offers to help, hand them a cup, not the river.

You'd be surprised how fast the cups add up. Not overnight. I won't lie to you about that. But a week of finished small jobs feels completely different from a week of half-finished big ones. You start to trust yourself again. And once you trust yourself, other people start to trust you too.

### What this looks like in a creative life

Let me make it real for a minute.

Say you're a writer with a catalogue and a circle of people around you. Maybe you write fiction under one name and non-fiction under another, the way I do. Maybe there's music in the picture, or friends whose work you want to lift alongside yours.

Your goal sits at the top of one page. Under it, the plan breaks into lanes: the books, the website, social media, the music, the people in your circle. Each lane has small jobs. A chapter draft. A cover brief. Three posts for the week. A short article like this one. A message to a collaborator. Every job has a name next to it and a clear picture of finished.

Each morning you, the lead, look at the page. What's done? What came back for another pass? What's stuck? You make one or two decisions, hand out what needs handing out, and then you do your own job, which is usually the writing. That's the part nobody else can do for you.

At the end of the week, you check. Not by feel. By the page. You can see exactly what moved and what didn't. And if something didn't move, you don't beat yourself up. You ask why, you make the job smaller or clearer, and you hand it out again.

That's it. That's the whole system. It's not complicated. It's just disciplined.

### The quiet that comes after

I'll leave you where we started, in that late-night quiet with the refrigerator humming.

Picture that same room a few months from now. The house is still quiet. But your head is quiet too. You know what the goal is because it's written down. You know what's moving because it's on one page. You know some of the work got done today by hands other than yours, and that it was checked, and that it's right. You're not carrying everything anymore. You're leading it.

That kind of peace isn't luck. It's built, one small finished job at a time.

You can start tonight. Get one sheet of paper. Write your goal at the top, plain enough that someone could check you on it. Under it, write the next five small jobs. Circle the one you'll finish tomorrow.

Then go to sleep. You've already started.
